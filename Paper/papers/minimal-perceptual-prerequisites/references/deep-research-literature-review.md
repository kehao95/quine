## Stigmergy and indirect coordination: definitions and known minimal conditions

The canonical definition in the stigmergy literature is that coordination occurs when a trace left by an action in a medium stimulates subsequent actions (by the same or other agent), enabling organized collective behavior without direct messaging. citeturn9view4turn13search14 The term originates with entity["people","Pierre-Paul Grass\u00e9","zoologist 1959 stigmergy"], and early life-sciences discussions explicitly emphasize that individuals can behave “as if alone” while the group-level outcome is coordinated. citeturn13search14

Two sources are particularly useful for your “minimal conditions” angle:

**Minimal agent/environment capabilities.** entity["people","Owen Holland","robotics researcher stigmergy"] and entity["people","Chris Melhuish","robotics researcher stigmergy"] propose an intentionally minimal lens: stigmergy requires (i) agents that can move through an environment and act on it, and (ii) an environment that can be changed locally by agents, with changes persisting long enough to affect later behavior. citeturn8view0 This very nearly reads like a checklist for your filesystem substrate: “movement” can be interpreted as agents traversing/actioning a shared namespace; “acting” is file creation/modification; “persistence” is the longevity of artifacts and their metadata/events.

**No mutual awareness requirement.** entity["people","Francis Heylighen","stigmergy researcher"] is exceptionally explicit that stigmergy can support complex coordination without planning, control, direct communication, simultaneous presence, **or even mutual awareness**. citeturn9view4turn7search1 This is the cleanest prior-art statement that “coordination without explicit agent awareness” is conceptually within stigmergy’s umbrella—even if most empirical systems do not operationalize *epistemic isolation* as strongly as you do.

A second boundary that matters is the distinction between **marker-based** stigmergy (agents deposit separate “signals,” e.g., pheromones) and **sematectonic** stigmergy (coordination emerges from modifications to the structure/work product itself). citeturn9view4turn13search14 Your “file handshake” and “division-of-labor declaration” look marker-based; but many shared-resource phenomena (unexpected budget depletion, file contention patterns, partial logs) are closer to sematectonic traces, because the “work product” and the “signal” are entangled in the same substrate.

## Where the stigmergy literature’s boundary likely is for your claim

The most supportive part of the stigmergy tradition for your novelty story is the repeated insistence that agents need not directly communicate or know one another, because the medium externalizes state and sequences work. citeturn9view4turn7search18turn13search14 That said, the stigmergy literature often treats “agents not needing to know each other” as a *description of mechanism*, not as an *experimental constraint on what the agent is told*. In typical ALIFE / swarm robotics instantiations, it is already common knowledge to the modeler (and often implicitly built into the agent controller design) that there are multiple agents, a shared medium, and a trace semantics. citeturn8view0turn15view0turn13search14

This creates a natural boundary line you can exploit:

In classical accounts (insects, swarms, optimization), the agent typically does **not** (and need not) infer “there exists another agent” as a distinct hypothesis. The behavior can remain purely reactive to local traces. citeturn13search14turn9view4 In your result, by contrast, the LLM agent appears to do something closer to **abduction over latent actors**: it observes anomalies (budget depletion, unexpected filesystem changes), generates a causal hypothesis (“another actor exists”), and then constructs a protocol to reduce conflict and exploit parallelism.

Even the strongest “no mutual awareness” statements in the stigmergy literature are compatible with your result, but they do not automatically imply it. Put differently: stigmergy shows that **mutual awareness is not necessary for coordination**, but your system shows that **a general-purpose agent can *recover* a representation of peers from minimal traces, even when that representation is not initially provided**. citeturn9view4turn8view0

There is also a useful bridge to *engineered indirect-coordination mechanisms* in multi-agent systems, where the “environment as coordination medium” is explicit. The environment-programming tradition (including blackboard-style coordination) argues that the environment can mediate and regulate agent interaction; a blackboard is a canonical mechanism for uncoupled communication among agents. citeturn8view1 This matters because it gives you formal language to say: your filesystem + resource counters form an **environmental coordination substrate**, but the key novelty is that agents were **not instructed to treat it as such**.

## Emergent protocols, zero-shot coordination, and epistemic assumptions

Your “self-invented protocols” component touches three adjacent literatures that help triangulate novelty: emergent communication, zero-shot coordination, and theory-of-mind/partner modeling.

Emergent communication in neural multi-agent work typically assumes either explicit communication channels or explicit interaction games in which “messages” are action variables. Examples include CommNet (learning continuous communication alongside policy learning) and differentiable/learned message passing in deep multi-agent RL. citeturn5search4turn5search1 Even when communication is “implicit,” it is often modeled as **agents interpreting other agents’ actions** rather than inferring existence from environmental side effects; for instance, “Learning to Communicate Implicitly by Actions” frames implicit communication as extracting information from teammates’ actions in imperfect-information settings. citeturn2search18

The zero-shot coordination (ZSC) tradition is directly relevant because it formalizes coordination without prior agreement with a specific partner. “Other-Play” defines ZSC as the setting in which agents must coordinate with a novel partner at test time. citeturn22search0 ZSC-Eval further positions ZSC as an out-of-distribution generalization challenge driven by distribution shift between training partners and deployment partners. citeturn22search5turn22search1

However, these ZSC formulations typically still assume (at least implicitly) that agents know they are in a multi-agent task. Recent work explicitly questions this assumption by attacking the “common knowledge” premise: a “Noisy ZSC” formulation argues that standard ZSC can be unrealistic because it assumes the coordination problem specification is common knowledge among agents, and proposes a variant where agents observe different noisy versions of the underlying problem. citeturn26view0turn26view1 This is a strong conceptual stepping stone for your “epistemic isolation” framing: you remove not only common knowledge of the exact coordination problem, but also **awareness that a partner exists at all**.

Critically, the very recent LLM-focused ZSC paper “Zero-Shot Coordination among LLM Agents” (workshop, 2026) argues that prior LLM coordination often relies on task-specific scaffolding and that frontier LLM agents fail to coordinate even in simplified ZSC environments; it proposes “Coordination Friendly Definitions” as a mechanism to improve robustness. citeturn21view1turn21view0 This gives you an up-to-date anchor for “the state of LLM coordination without heavy scaffolding is still fragile,” while your result provides an existence proof that—under a different information structure—LLMs can bootstrap coordination via environmental signals.

On theory of mind (ToM), the landscape is mixed. In cooperative games like entity["video_game","Hanabi","cooperative card game"], the challenge paper emphasizes reasoning about other agents’ beliefs and intentions (ToM-like skills) as central to success under imperfect information and constrained communication. citeturn22search25turn22search21 In LLM-agent settings, “Theory of Mind for Multi-Agent Collaboration via Large Language Models” reports evidence of emergent collaborative behavior and ToM capabilities in a cooperative text game, and finds that explicit belief-state representations can improve performance and ToM inference accuracy. citeturn24view0turn23search0 Yet the stigmergy framing reminds reviewers that ToM is not a universal prerequisite: if coordination is mediated by traces in a medium, complex organization can arise without agents explicitly representing others’ mental states. citeturn9view4turn8view0

If you want a formal vocabulary for “agents lacking awareness of key parts of the interaction situation,” the game-theory literature on *games with unawareness* provides one: it models situations where players’ subjective views may omit actions/players and analyzes equilibrium behavior under such awareness constraints. citeturn6search0 While this is not an ALIFE staple, it can help you name the gap precisely as *unawareness/discovery* rather than “no communication.”

## Novelty positioning and defensible claims for an ALIFE submission

A defensible novelty claim for ALIFE that aligns with prior literature while preserving your key contribution is easiest to express as a three-axis delta over existing work.

The “minimal stigmergic conditions” axis is well-supported prior art: stigmergy can operate with minimal agent capabilities (act on environment; environment changes persist) and without mutual awareness or direct communication. citeturn8view0turn9view4turn7search1 Therefore, claiming novelty as “coordination through environmental traces” *alone* will likely be challenged as incremental, because both stigmergy and blackboard approaches already occupy that space. citeturn8view1turn9view3

Your strongest novelty is instead the “epistemic isolation → peer inference → protocol invention” axis:

Stigmergy’s historical motivation is that insects can behave “as if alone” while the group outcome is coordinated. citeturn13search14 Your system recreates the “as if alone” constraint in a way that is unusually explicit for modern AI (agents not told peers exist), and then shows that general-purpose LLM agents can (i) infer hidden peers from *anomalous environmental observations* and (ii) invent coordination artifacts that stabilize collective behavior. That explicitly connects ALIFE-style emergence to cognitive agent inference—something classical swarm systems often do not test, because they do not endow agents with open-ended hypothesis formation and language-based protocol construction. citeturn8view0turn9view4turn2search18

The “LLM multi-agent coordination without explicit channels” axis is also strong:

Mainstream LLM multi-agent systems overwhelmingly rely on explicit inter-agent messaging (conversation) or explicit role/structure scaffolding (profiles, SOPs, orchestration hierarchies), as emphasized both in surveys and in flagship frameworks like AutoGen, CAMEL, MetaGPT, AgentVerse, and MegaAgent. citeturn8view2turn11view0turn8view4turn16view1turn17view1turn8view6 Even when a shared workspace or blackboard is used, it is typically treated as a first-class communication substrate where agents intentionally post semantically meaningful requests and responses. citeturn9view3turn8view3 Your result differs in that coordination bootstraps from channels that are *not declared as communication* (filesystem event traces; budget anomalies), which makes it closer to stigmergy’s “trace semantics emerge from use” than to “messages were allowed, just not via chat.”

Finally, the “zero-shot coordination gap” axis offers a timely contrast:

ZSC in multi-agent RL assumes coordination with unseen partners but usually presupposes the existence of partners and often presupposes shared problem specification; recent work explicitly critiques these assumptions and proposes variants that relax common knowledge. citeturn22search0turn26view0turn26view1 In the LLM setting, the 2026 ZSC paper reports that frontier LLM agents can fail even in simplified ZSC environments and proposes additional scaffolding (“coordination friendly definitions”) to help. citeturn21view1turn21view0 Your contribution can be positioned as complementary and orthogonal: rather than improving LLM ZSC via prompt definitions, you show that **a different information structure**—stigmergic traces and resource perturbations—can enable robust coordination even without explicit partner modeling or explicit communication channels.
