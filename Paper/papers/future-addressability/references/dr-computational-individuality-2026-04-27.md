## A Conceptual Map of Identity Criteria

To contextualize future addressability as a continuity criterion, we must first map the theoretical landscape of identity. Identity is not a monolith; it is parsed through varying lenses depending on the systemic priorities of the observer. The following conceptual map outlines the dominant criteria used to anchor identity, serving as the backdrop against which future addressability will be evaluated.

### Summary Comparison Matrix

| Identity Criterion | Core Definition | Primary Domain | Key Limitation in Computation | Relevance to Future Addressability |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Artifact** | Grounded in physical/structural persistence. | Hardware Engineering | Obsolete in distributed cloud environments. | Antithetical; ignores functional reachability. |
| **Source** | Defined by original codebase or creator genesis. | IP / Provenance | Fails to account for runtime state evolution. | Irrelevant; purely backwards-looking. |

The landscape of identity criteria can be broadly categorized into physical, historical, functional, and relational domains:

*   **Artifact**: This criterion grounds identity in physical or structural persistence. An entity is continuous if the material substrate—such as a specific server rack or localized microprocessor—remains intact. In modern distributed computing, where virtual machines migrate seamlessly across global data centers, the artifact criterion is generally considered obsolete.
*   **Source**: Identity is defined by origin or genesis. A program retains its identity because it descends from a specific codebase, compiler, or creator. While useful for intellectual property and provenance, source identity fails to account for runtime evolution or state changes.
*   **Memory**: This criterion anchors individuality in retained state or historical logs. An entity is defined by its accumulated data. If a computational agent retains a continuous ledger of its past states, it is considered the same individual. However, as memory can be cloned or bifurcated, memory alone cannot resolve branching identities.
*   **Process**: Rooted in process ontology, this view treats identity as an ongoing, spatiotemporally extended occurrence rather than a static snapshot. An individual is a dynamic unfolding of events. A computation is continuous if there is a causal, temporal flux connecting state A to state B. However, this criterion fails to account for paused or serialized computations (e.g., a frozen virtual machine state saved to a disk) where the causal flux halts temporarily but logical identity is expected to persist in modern computing environments.
*   **Endpoint**: A teleological criterion where identity is defined by a final goal. An agent's identity is continuous so long as it is pursuing the same functional objective, such as calculating a specific prime number or resolving a discrete user query, regardless of the intermediate steps taken. This approach is limited in modern computing because it fails to account for daemon processes, continuous event loops, or generalized orchestrators that have no discrete terminal endpoint but clearly maintain an ongoing identity.
*   **Behavior**: Distinct from the endpoint, this criterion focuses on input/output equivalence. If a successor module processes inputs and yields outputs indistinguishable from its predecessor, it is deemed continuous. This is heavily utilized in semantic views of computation (theories positing that physical systems compute only by virtue of representing semantic content or meaning). In distributed computing, this criterion's primary limitation is that it fails to distinguish between an original process and a functionally identical clone running in parallel, which is critical for accurate network orchestration.
*   **Obligation**: Identity is formed by anticipated fulfillment. An entity exists to resolve a commitment made in the past. In programming, this is operationalized through "futures" and "promises"—placeholders for values that do not yet exist but must eventually be computed and returned. The key limitation here is fragility: if a system is designed to deliberately drop obligations during load shedding (such as ignoring promises to preserve memory), its identity becomes brittle and theoretically ceases during high-stress network partitions, despite the physical application remaining intact.
*   **Carrier**: The systemic medium or infrastructural conduit that transfers state or routing data. If a message can successfully traverse a network fabric to reach a node, the continuity of the carrier validates the continuity of the receiver. In modern cloud architecture, this criterion is heavily limited by highly decoupled message queues (like Apache Kafka) where the carrier routing is abstracted entirely away from the recipient's specific logical identity, meaning carrier continuity does not guarantee receiver continuity.
*   **Organization**: Identity is maintained through the closure of constraints, a concept heavily utilized in biological autopoiesis. An organization is continuous if the current network of interacting processes dynamically presupposes, and was caused by, an earlier state of that same closed network. When mapped strictly to digital computation, this fails because computational systems lack thermodynamic necessity and metabolic self-production; rendering the concept of closed "organization" merely an external metaphor rather than an intrinsic constraint.

Synthesizing this map reveals the strength of your proposed thesis. **Future addressability** is not an isolated metric; it elegantly intertwines *Organization*, *Carrier*, and *Obligation*. It posits that an individual is defined not by looking backward at its *Source* or *Memory*, but by looking forward: it maintains *Organization* specifically by ensuring that the *Carrier* can deliver an *Obligation* to it. If the ecology cannot address the entity to fulfill an obligation, the computational individual has, for all pragmatic purposes, ceased to exist. 

## Arguments for Future Addressability as an Operational Continuity Criterion

The thesis that individuality is indexed by future addressability finds deep, cross-disciplinary support. By moving the locus of identity from structural persistence to relational reachability, the thesis successfully resolves several paradoxes inherent in distributed computing and process philosophy.

### The Actor Model: Decoupling Addressability from Material Identity
The strongest structural support for future addressability comes from the Actor model of concurrent computation. In 1973, Carl E. Hewitt, Peter Bishop, and Richard Steiger published the seminal paper *A Universal Modular ACTOR Formalism for Artificial Intelligence* in the Proceedings of the International Joint Conference on Artificial Intelligence [cite: 1, 2]. They proposed an architecture composed entirely of "actors"—independent, asynchronous entities that communicate exclusively by passing messages [cite: 3, 4]. 

Crucially, in the Actor model, actors do not have an inherently visible "identity" or shared memory; they only possess **addresses** [cite: 5, 6, 7]. An address acts as a capability: if an ecology possesses an actor's address, it can send a message to it [cite: 5, 6]. As Hewitt noted, an address does not reveal whether a single physical actor or a distributed proxy of millions of actors is receiving the message [cite: 5, 6]. Thus, operational continuity in an Actor system is defined purely by whether the address remains valid and reachable. If an actor's address becomes unreachable, the actor can no longer influence the future of the computation [cite: 6, 8]. 

This directly supports the thesis: an actor's continuity is preserved only if its future obligations (messages) can reach it through the relevant carrier (the address) [cite: 5, 9]. This logic was further extended in Henry G. Baker and Carl Hewitt's 1977 paper, *The Incremental Garbage Collection of Processes*, published in *ACM SIGPLAN Notices* [cite: 10, 11, 12]. Baker and Hewitt demonstrated that a process (an actor) is only conceptually "alive" if it is reachable [cite: 8, 13]. If a process can no longer be addressed, it is "garbage," regardless of whether it is still actively spinning in local memory [cite: 8, 14]. They explicitly linked this to call-by-future mechanisms (mechanisms where variables are bound to concurrent processes evaluating future arguments before they are strictly needed) [cite: 14, 15]. Therefore, computational survival—and by extension, individuality—is strictly a function of future addressability [cite: 14, 15].

### Promises, Futures, and Teleological Continuity
Closely related to the Actor model is the programmatic paradigm of "futures" and "promises," concepts that inherently operationalize your notion of *obligations*. The history of these constructs perfectly mirrors the thesis. 

Daniel P. Friedman and David S. Wise formalized the concept in their 1978 paper, *Aspects of Applicative Programming for Parallel Processing* (IEEE Transactions on Computers), followed swiftly by Baker and Hewitt's 1977 integration of "futures" [cite: 10, 16, 17]. In 1988, Barbara Liskov and Liuba Shrira popularized the term "promises" in their paper *Promises: Linguistic Support for Asynchronous RPC in Argus*, illustrating how promises serve as placeholders for values that will exist in the future (where RPC stands for Remote Procedure Call, a protocol where a program executes a procedure in another address space) [cite: 10, 15, 18]. 

A promise is, quite literally, a computational obligation [cite: 18, 19]. An individual process maintains continuity across asynchronous, distributed space precisely because it holds the responsibility to "resolve" or "fulfill" a promise sent by a carrier [cite: 10, 19]. The successor state of the process is only meaningful relative to the future ecology waiting for that promise to resolve [cite: 10, 18]. If the ecological link is broken—if the promise is dropped—the process loses its relational identity and is terminated [cite: 18].

### Genidentity: Genesis Over Substance
To translate this from computer science into philosophy, we must examine the concept of *genidentity* (Genidentität). Originally formulated by Kurt Lewin in his 1922 habilitation treatise, *Der Begriff der Genese in Physik, Biologie und Entwicklungsgeschichte* (The Concept of Genesis in Physics, Biology, and Evolutionary History), genidentity provides a framework for understanding identity through continuous change [cite: 20, 21, 22]. 

Lewin argued that two objects (or states) are identical across time not because they share the same physical properties, but because they share an existential relationship of genesis: one developed from the other [cite: 20, 21]. The philosopher Hans Reichenbach later formalized genidentity in physics (e.g., in *The Direction of Time*, 1956), and Thomas Pradeu recently adapted it to biology in his 2018 book chapter, *Genidentity and Biological Processes* (in *Everything Flows: Towards a Processual Philosophy of Biology*), showing how identity requires following a well-identified series of continuous states [cite: 23, 24, 25].

Applying genidentity to your thesis yields a powerful result. If computational identity is not about physical substance, it must be about the continuity of processual states [cite: 26, 27]. Genidentity dictates that State B is continuous with State A because B arose from A to carry forward an ongoing process [cite: 23, 28]. By introducing *future addressability*, you answer the critical question of *how* we track genidentity in computation: the genesis is validated by the continuity of the address. The successor state (B) is genidentical to the predecessor state (A) because B inherits the carrier addresses and obligations established by A [cite: 6, 24].

### The Information Theory of Individuality
Support for the ecological aspect of your thesis—that identity relies on a "future ecology"—can be found in David Krakauer, Nils Bertschinger, Eckehard Olbrich, Jessica C. Flack, and Nihat Ay's 2020 paper, *The Information Theory of Individuality* (ITI), published in *Theory in Biosciences* [cite: 29, 30, 31]. 

Krakauer and his colleagues argue that individuality is not a binary, bounded physical trait, but a continuous information-theoretic process [cite: 30, 32, 33]. They propose that individuals are "aggregates that preserve a measure of temporal integrity, i.e., 'propagate' information from their past into their futures" [cite: 30, 31]. Crucially, they define individuality through a "figure-ground" Gestalt approach, where the individual is separated from its environment by how it orders information to reduce temporal uncertainty in the future [cite: 30, 31, 34]. 

Your thesis directly mirrors ITI. By defining continuity as the ability to be reached by "future obligations," you are describing an entity that successfully propagates its informational structure into the future, maintaining its figure-ground distinction against the ecology [cite: 30, 35]. If it cannot be addressed in the future ecology, it ceases to propagate information and dissolves back into the environmental "ground" [cite: 30, 36]. 

### Grounding in Reality: Erlang/OTP Supervisor Trees
To bridge this theory with operational reality, consider the architecture of Erlang and its Open Telecom Platform (OTP), a robust environment famous for high-concurrency and fault-tolerance. Erlang operationalizes future addressability through its "supervision trees" [cite: 37, 38].

In an Erlang OTP application, computational workers do not maintain physical persistence. They frequently crash, losing all local memory and state [cite: 39, 40]. However, the *identity* of the service is preserved by a Supervisor. The Supervisor monitors its worker nodes, and if a worker dies, it dynamically spawns a new one [cite: 37, 38]. In this ecosystem, a process's identity is intrinsically tied to its `pid` (process identifier)—which acts solely as an address to where messages (obligations) should be sent [cite: 37, 39]. 

When a dynamic child is added to a supervisor tree, or restarted after a crash, it is assigned a new `pid`. To the future ecology, the system ensures that the *role* remains addressable. The system survives not because the artifact or memory survived, but because the supervisor dynamically reinstantiates the entity to ensure it can receive future messages [cite: 38, 40]. Identity, here, is quite literally the capacity of the supervisor to maintain the system's addressable obligations in the face of physical ephemerality.

### Practical Identity and the Unification of Agency
Finally, the concept of "obligations" anchoring identity finds profound resonance in the philosophy of identity, specifically through Christine Korsgaard's concept of *practical identity*. In her 1996 book, *The Sources of Normativity* (and related lectures on *The Authority of Reflection*), Korsgaard argues that our identity is a description under which we value ourselves and find our actions worth undertaking [cite: 41, 42, 43]. 

For Korsgaard, human identity is inherently forward-looking and binds us to obligations. We require a unified identity over time because we have "one life to lead" and must make plans [cite: 42, 43]. Identity is thus constructed by practical reason: our commitments and obligations to our future selves and ecologies force us to act as a continuous agent [cite: 42, 44]. 

Translating this to computational individuality, a system maintains "practical identity" because it has algorithmic plans to execute. The "obligation" to act on an addressed message forces the computational system to preserve a unified state across time [cite: 6, 42]. Without an obligation to a future state, the need for a unified identity collapses. 

## Strongest Objections and Limits

While the thesis is highly innovative, it runs headfirst into several entrenched philosophical and biological roadblocks. Any robust defense of the thesis must anticipate and address these criticisms.

### The Biological Divide: Autopoiesis vs. Algorithmic Mimicry
The most formidable objection arises from the philosophy of biology and artificial life (ALife). Theorists in these fields argue that true individuality—often termed "organizational continuity"—requires thermodynamic openness and *autopoiesis* (self-creation), which computational systems fundamentally lack. 

In their 2015 book, *Biological Autonomy: A Philosophical and Theoretical Enquiry* (Springer), Alvaro Moreno and Matteo Mossio present an organizational account of living systems [cite: 45, 46, 47]. They argue that biological autonomy is grounded in "organizational closure"—a self-maintaining network of processes where constraints channel energy and matter to constantly rebuild the system's own parts [cite: 45, 46, 48]. James DiFrisco and Matteo Mossio further developed this in their 2020 book chapter, *Diachronic Identity in Complex Life Cycles: An Organizational Perspective*, arguing that diachronic identity is maintained only if a system's current closed organization dynamically derives from its previous instantiations and realizes a continuous succession of constraint closures [cite: 49, 50, 51].

Critics applying this to your thesis will argue that computational "future addressability" is merely extrinsic, algorithmic mimicry [cite: 52, 53]. An organism maintains organizational continuity because it *must* harvest energy to stave off entropy; it has intrinsic goals and a genuine stake in its survival [cite: 50, 52, 54]. An algorithm, however, is hermetically sealed from thermodynamic consequences. If a computational node drops an address or fails to fulfill an obligation, it does not "die" in a metabolic sense [cite: 53, 55]. Therefore, defining computational continuity purely via addressability conflates true, internally driven agency with externally imposed, syntactical routing rules [cite: 53, 55]. Addressability is a network property, not an index of biological-grade individuality [cite: 55, 56]. 

### The Semantic vs. Mechanistic Gap
A second major objection lies within the philosophy of computation, specifically in the debate over how to individuate physical computing systems. The field is split between the Semantic View (SV) and the Mechanistic View (MV) [cite: 57]. 

Gualtiero Piccinini, a leading proponent of the mechanistic view (e.g., *Computationalism in the Philosophy of Mind*, 2009; *Physical Computation*, 2015), argues that computation is individuated by the lawful, mechanistic transformations of medium-independent digits, regardless of what those digits represent [cite: 57, 58]. Conversely, the Semantic View insists that "there is no computation without representation" [cite: 58, 59]. 

Your thesis—anchoring continuity on "future obligations" and "ecology"—leans heavily into the semantic and functional realm. An objection from strict mechanists would assert that "obligations" are subjective interpretations imposed by an external observer or programmer [cite: 57, 58]. Mechanistically, a processor is simply changing voltage states [cite: 58]. If addressability is merely a semantic overlay, then it fails as a rigorous, objective criterion for physical computational identity [cite: 57, 58]. It risks identifying the computation based on what the user *wants* the system to do (the obligation), rather than what the physical mechanism is actually doing [cite: 58, 59].

### Triviality and the Ephemerality of the Carrier
The thesis must grapple with the technical realities of distributed systems and "triviality arguments" in the philosophy of mind (such as those advanced by Hilary Putnam and David Chalmers) [cite: 59]. Triviality arguments suggest that, under loose mapping constraints, almost any physical system can be said to implement any computation [cite: 58, 59]. If "addressability" is the only criterion, the definition of an individual becomes dangerously promiscuous [cite: 59]. 

Furthermore, in virtual actor models (such as Microsoft's Orleans or Dapr, where Dapr is a Distributed Application Runtime that provides portable, event-driven architecture for microservices), an actor's state is frequently offloaded to external persistent storage and its physical address is destroyed to save resources [cite: 4]. When a new message (an obligation) arrives, the system activates a perfectly identical clone of the actor on a totally different machine [cite: 4]. If the carrier routing changes, and the physical memory location changes, is it the same individual? The future addressability thesis says *yes*, because the obligation reached it [cite: 4]. However, critics argue this reduces identity to mere data replication, collapsing the distinction between an individual and a clone [cite: 4, 5]. If multiple proxy addresses forward to the same actor, or if one address multicasts to a swarm of identical actors, the 1-to-1 mapping of individuality shatters [cite: 5, 6].

### Intentional Unaddressability and the Dormancy Paradox
A final necessary consideration addresses systems engineered to be explicitly unaddressable. How does this thesis account for the identity of computational systems that intentionally isolate, rotate, or terminate their addresses for security purposes (e.g., Moving Target Defense, ephemeral zero-trust nodes) or for long-term dormancy (air-gapped archival nodes)?

If individuality requires an active carrier path to receive obligations, an ephemeral zero-trust node would theoretically "die" and be replaced by a new individual every time its IP dynamically shifts. However, in these paradigms, the "future ecology" incorporates the scheduled latency or ledger-based routing of the system. While the specific address rotates, the orchestrator's capability to update the ledger ensures the entity remains *latently* addressable to authorized carriers. Similarly, for air-gapped archival systems, the "obligation" is merely deferred rather than severed. Under the thesis, these systems maintain their individuality precisely because their intentional unaddressability is a programmatic delay mechanism engineered to ensure they can securely fulfill future obligations when reconnected.

## Manuscript-Safe Language: Presenting the Thesis

To navigate these objections, the thesis must be presented as an *operational criterion* rather than an ontological universal law. The goal is to provide a pragmatic framework for distributed and agentic systems, without necessarily claiming to solve the metaphysical hard problem of identity. 

When preparing this thesis for a manuscript, adopt a tone of epistemic humility and pragmatic utility. The following phrasings and modular templates are recommended for framing the thesis safely:

**1. Positioning as an Evaluative Heuristic**
> *"Rather than asserting a rigid metaphysical ontology of digital persistence, this paper posits future addressability as an operational heuristic for computational individuality. By defining continuity relative to a future ecology, we provide a pragmatic criterion for evaluating agentic persistence in distributed, asynchronous environments."*

**2. Acknowledging the Biological Limit**
> *"While biological autopoiesis anchors individuality in thermodynamic openness and metabolic constraint closure, distributed computational systems demand a distinctly synthetic criterion. We suggest that in the absence of metabolic necessity, computational individuality is effectively indexed by its capacity to sustain relational obligations across time. Therefore, future addressability acts as the computational analog to biological organizational continuity."*

**3. Framing the Mechanism of Continuity**
> *"We develop the thesis that a successor state is continuous with its predecessor only relative to a specific future ecology. Specifically, continuity is maintained if pending systemic obligations can reliably reach the successor through a relevant carrier. In this model, identity is not a static footprint stored in memory, but a dynamic, forward-looking vector defined by the system's capacity to be acted upon."*

**4. Decoupling from Structural Essentialism**
> *"Drawing on the decoupling of identity and routing found in the Actor model, our framework suggests that structural persistence is subordinate to functional reachability. A computational individual survives not by maintaining the integrity of its physical substrate, but by preserving the integrity of its addresses and its capacity to resolve future computational promises."*

## Conclusion

The thesis that computational individuality is indexed by future addressability offers a highly generative framework for modern computer science and philosophy. It elegantly discards outdated notions of hardware-bound artifacts, replacing them with a relational, processual model of identity. 

By grounding this concept in Carl Hewitt's Actor model and the computational mechanics of promises and futures, the thesis possesses strong technical validity. When enriched by Kurt Lewin's genidentity, Korsgaard's practical identity, and Krakauer's information theory of individuality, it becomes a philosophically rigorous construct. 

While it faces necessary friction from biological purists who demand thermodynamic openness for true autonomy, and from mechanistic philosophers wary of semantic overlays, these objections can be managed by properly scoping the claim. By presenting future addressability as an operational criterion—a pragmatic lens through which to understand continuity in asynchronous, distributed networks—you establish a powerful new paradigm for evaluating the persistence of digital agents in an increasingly fluid computational landscape.
