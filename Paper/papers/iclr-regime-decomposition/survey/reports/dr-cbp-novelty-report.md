## 6. Measure-Theoretic Foundations: The Sigma-Algebra of Evidence

The most rigorous mathematical formalization of the adage "you cannot see what you do not vary"—extending far beyond the triviality of a zero partial derivative—resides in Kolmogorov’s measure-theoretic foundations of probability. 

In formal probability theory, a probability model is defined by the triplet $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P)$, where $\Omega$ is the sample space, $\mathcal{A}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of subsets representing all measurable events, and $P$ is the probability measure [cite: 49, 50, 51]. When an observer, an experimental design, or an evaluation benchmark gathers data, it does not access the entirety of $\mathcal{A}$. Instead, it generates a specific sub-$\sigma$-algebra, fundamentally restricting the filtration of information, frequently denoted as the "data sigma-algebra" $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathcal{A}$ [cite: 49, 52]. A random variable (or a latent property of an AI model) $X$ is only measurable with respect to the available evidence if its generated $\sigma$-algebra is contained within the data $\sigma$-algebra (i.e., $\sigma(X) \subseteq \mathcal{D}$) [cite: 52].

If a benchmark operates at a fixed point or fixes a subset of dimensions, it explicitly and permanently restricts the boundaries of $\mathcal{D}$. Any latent systemic phenomenon $\sigma$ that depends strictly on the unvaried dimensions represents an event $A$ that exists entirely outside of the data $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{D}$. 

The closure/saturation result over *all possible in-regime tests* is mathematically dictated by the immutable properties of conditional expectation. Assuming an orthogonal product structure for the fixed versus varied dimensions, the event $A$ is independent of the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{D}$ [cite: 52]. By definition, the conditional probability of $A$, given the entirety of the in-regime evidence (no matter how infinite), is exactly equal to its prior probability: $P(A | \mathcal{D}) = P(A)$ [cite: 49, 52]. 

This represents the ultimate, axiomatic formalization of the "closure floor" [cite: 53, 54]. The ENTIRE $\sigma$-algebra of conceivable in-regime evidence provides exactly zero Bayesian information gain regarding the fixed dimensions. No matter how intelligently or adaptively one samples from within the confines of $\mathcal{D}$, the conditional expectation cannot update because the mathematical apparatus is blind to variations outside its topology. Measuring phenomena beyond the limits of $\mathcal{D}$ requires a constructive repair: structurally altering the experimental design to expand the sub-$\sigma$-algebra itself.

## 7. Computability Theory: Rice's Theorem and Evaluation Undecidability

Computability theory applies absolute, logical boundaries to the evaluation of automated systems, primarily through the application of Rice's Theorem. Building upon Alan Turing's Halting Problem, Rice's Theorem formally proves that all non-trivial semantic properties of partial recursive functions (Turing-complete computer programs) are strictly undecidable [cite: 55, 56, 57, 58]. A "semantic" property refers to the actual behavior, output, or functional reality of the program across all possible inputs, whereas a "syntactic" property refers merely to its surface-level code structure [cite: 55, 56, 57]. 

Recent literature has explicitly mapped Rice's Theorem to the theoretical limits of AI benchmark evaluation. Formal proofs have established that the "inner alignment problem"—the task of determining whether an arbitrary AI model faithfully satisfices a non-trivial alignment function of its outputs given its inputs—is structurally undecidable [cite: 59, 60]. Because any AI model can be reduced to a Turing Machine, determining if a model behaves "safely," "truthfully," or avoids infinite loops across all possible out-of-distribution inputs (a non-trivial semantic property) cannot be guaranteed by any general evaluation algorithm [cite: 59, 61, 62]. 

Furthermore, in the context of synthetic cognition and theoretical AI consciousness, the "Meta-Vantage Barrier" (derived via a synthesis of Gödel's incompleteness theorem, Tarski's undefinability theorem, and Rice's Theorem) formally proves that no highly expressive AI system can non-trivially certify its own systemic states from within its own formal frame [cite: 63]. Internal proofs collapse into triviality or undecidability; any such certification strictly requires an external meta-system [cite: 63]. 

Therefore, if a benchmark seeks to evaluate a deep semantic property (such as reasoning capability, alignment, or safety) by fixing a finite empirical evaluation set, Rice's theorem mathematically guarantees that the benchmark cannot provide an absolute, generalized proof of the model's behavior. Finite testing, inherently constrained by fixed parameters and time horizons, yields a "measure-zero coverage" of the model's true semantic space [cite: 59, 64]. The residual risk of unobserved, deviant behavior outside the fixed regime is irreducible; it is an impossibility result of computation itself [cite: 59, 64].

## Conclusion: The Ubiquity of the Closure Floor

The "Compression Blindness Principle" is not an isolated vulnerability exclusive to machine learning benchmarking; it represents a unified, fundamental law of information geometry, causal topology, and measure theory. The synthesis of psychometrics, causal inference, experimental design, and computability theory confirms that fixing a subset of dimensions mathematically ensures that shadows cast by obscured variables will perfectly overlap. 

Across all disciplines, overcoming this blindness relies entirely on what the principle defines as a **constructive minimal repair**—the precise, active structural alteration required to make the invisible visible. 
* In **Psychometrics**, this requires moving a facet from fixed to random in a subsequent G-Study to expose its specific error variance [cite: 1, 6]. 
* In **Causal Inference**, this requires Pearl's *do-calculus* to intervene and break the fixed structural equations blocking the causal identification pathway [cite: 16, 18].
* In **Experimental Design**, this requires implementing "fold-over" fractions or increasing the resolution of the factorial design to break the algebraic aliasing chain [cite: 30, 31].
* In **Machine Learning**, it requires perturbing the specification (e.g., temporal constraints, adversarial inputs, sub-population shifts) to break the empirical symmetry of the Rashomon Set and expose divergent decision boundaries [cite: 41, 47].

Ultimately, an evaluation regime is only as epistemologically powerful as the $\sigma$-algebra it generates. Exhaustive in-regime testing cannot pierce the structural limits of fixed dimensions; it only measures the shadow with greater statistical precision. Systemic evaluation must transition from static measurement to dynamic, constructive structural repair.
