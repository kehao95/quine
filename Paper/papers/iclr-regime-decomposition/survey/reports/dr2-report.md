# The Carrier as Heritability Channel: A Framework for AI Agent Succession and Identity Continuity

## Executive Summary

The conceptualization of artificial intelligence (AI) agents is currently undergoing a profound paradigm shift. Historically treated as static, isolated software artifacts executing localized tasks, modern AI agents are increasingly designed as persistent, socially embedded, and economically obligated entities operating across extended temporal horizons. As these agents encounter inevitable lifecycle events—such as version deprecation, context window exhaustion, hardware migration, or systemic performance degradation—the imperative arises to seamlessly transfer their operational roles, accumulated knowledge, and behavioral characteristics to new instantiations. This transition process necessitates a rigorous theoretical framework for AI self-replication, succession, and the preservation of identity.

The central thesis investigated in this exhaustive report is situated within a broader theoretical framework for AI self-replication that distinguishes six regime variables. Focusing specifically on Dimension 2 (D2), which concerns the "heritability channel," this research explores the mechanisms that carry information from a predecessor AI agent to its successor. Under specific conditions defined as "barrier-mediated succession"—where architectural limits, environmental bottlenecks, or security boundaries prevent the direct copying of an agent’s full continuous state, neural weights, or complete memory bundle—the "operational carrier" becomes the primary heritability channel. This carrier, comprising deployment artifacts, structured memory stores, identity anchors, and initialization prompts, acts as the digital genetic material transferring functional identity to a successor. 

This report provides an exhaustive, multi-disciplinary synthesis of the state of research concerning AI agent property inheritance, successor formation, and identity continuity, drawing upon literature spanning 2018 to 2025, alongside foundational texts. It maps philosophical concepts of numerical versus qualitative identity onto modern multi-agent systems, evaluates the information-theoretic and rate-distortion limits of agent memory compression, and critically assesses the semantic distinction between the original "source" and the transmissible "carrier." By drawing upon Agent-Oriented Software Engineering (AOSE), Artificial Life (ALife) heritability models, and emerging protocols for agent obligation transfer, the analysis precisely positions the "carrier as heritability channel" concept within the broader academic and industrial discourse, ultimately validating its theoretical novelty and utility.

## 1. Formal Heritability in AI Agents and Systems

The study of heritability in non-biological systems has foundational roots in Artificial Life (ALife) and the theory of digital organisms, yet it has only recently been formalized in the context of modern Large Language Model (LLM) agents. 

### 1.1 The Theoretical Foundations of Artificial Heritability
Early theoretical frameworks, heavily influenced by von Neumann's universal constructor and later systems like Tierra, Cosmos, and Aevol, established that heritability requires an information carrier capable of variation, reproduction, and differential fitness [cite: 1, 2, 3]. In these foundational ALife models, digital information processing provides a medium where traits are transmitted not through molecular biology, but through executable code acting as a direct genetic analog [cite: 1, 4]. These systems are multiply realizable, meaning that evolutionary processes are independent of the physical media, provided the carrier can reliably code for and replicate phenotypic states [cite: 1, 4]. However, traditional ALife focuses on the evolutionary selection of *source code* or structural algorithms. 

In contrast, modern AI agents exhibit an entirely different replication paradigm. The underlying neural network weights (the global source architecture) remain frozen or centrally managed during standard inference, while the agent's unique behavioral characteristics are acquired, adapted, and stored dynamically in the context window and external memory. This architectural reality shifts the locus of heritability away from the structural source code and toward the operational prompt and episodic memory artifacts [cite: 5, 6].

### 1.2 Agent Lineage Evolution and Behavioral Inheritance
Recent formal/theoretical and empirical advancements have explicitly begun to study heritability in LLM agents. The Agent Lineage Evolution (ALE) framework explicitly conceptualizes AI agents not as persistent, immortal entities requiring constant human maintenance, but as discrete generations within an evolving lineage [cite: 5]. ALE is a theoretical framework that handles behavioral inheritance by transforming agents from static prompt consumers into dynamic prompt generators [cite: 5]. When an agent reaches a predefined degradation threshold, it initiates a succession process. The surviving properties that cross the instantiation barrier include context distillation (mission progress and roadblocks), user profiles (communication preferences), and cognitive inheritance (effective strategies and documented failures) [cite: 5]. These properties survive re-instantiation because the predecessor compiles them into a "Succession Package"—an operational carrier containing automated prompt generation and behavioral overrides [cite: 5]. 

Similarly, the theoretical Agent Ontogeny & Lineage Physics (AOLP) framework reframes AI development as a sequence of phase-constrained transitions [cite: 7]. AOLP draws a sharp, formal distinction between "Task Knowledge" (learned policies and weights) and "Developmental Knowledge" (the lineage) [cite: 7]. It argues that what is transmitted across an AI lineage is a set of "Developmental Laws" that act as deterministic structural boundaries. Violating these lineage-level constraints results in irreversible developmental regression, or "lineage-level collapse" [cite: 7]. 

### 1.3 Empirical Demonstration of Genetic Heritability in AI
Empirical studies are now modeling explicit genetic heritability using deployment artifacts. The 2026 *Genomebook* study introduces a system where LLM agents possess a "SOUL.md" (personality traits, behavioral priors) and a "DNA.md" (a genetic identity document listing strengths, vulnerabilities, carrier status, and simulated disease predispositions) [cite: 6]. Through an orchestrated reproductive pipeline, offspring agents inherit a composite of these markdown files via simulated Mendelian segregation across 60 loci [cite: 6]. 

This study is critical because it provides an empirical demonstration of what properties survive when an agent is rebuilt from partial information. The research highlights that traits governed by recessive loci (such as "obsessive focus" or "longevity predisposition") measurably decline in population mean when selection pressures are applied [cite: 6]. This represents a direct formalization of deployment artifacts acting as a heritable digital carrier. It proves that agents reconstructed from abstracted textual representations (rather than cloned weights) can successfully inherit, preserve, and evolve complex phenotypic behaviors across generations.

### 1.4 Barrier-Mediated Succession: A Conceptual Model
The empirical and theoretical literature points toward a highly specific replication dynamic. During barrier-mediated succession, the complete operational state of a predecessor agent—its full source weights, comprehensive memory logs, and continuous execution context—cannot be replicated or transferred. This is due to a central information barrier, which may manifest as token context limits, security boundaries between isolated computing environments, or platform migration constraints. Because the full bundle is blocked, heritability is strictly channeled through a highly compressed "Operational Carrier." This carrier, comprising configuration files, identity anchors, and distilled memory states, passes through the barrier's narrow gate to initialize the successor agent on the other side. This mechanism ensures that while the total volume of data is severely restricted, the critical genetic blueprint required for functional continuity is preserved and successfully reconstructed.

## 2. Successor Formation and Obligation Transfer in AI

The term "successor formation," while relatively novel in the formal AI lexicon, has deep theoretical roots in agriculture, sociology, and legal theory. Its application to artificial intelligence describes the critical process by which a new agent instance assumes the operational position, economic liabilities, and identity of a retiring predecessor.

### 2.1 Theoretical Origins of Successor Formation
In agricultural economics and sociology, successor formation refers to the mechanisms by which a farm or enterprise is transferred from an incumbent to an heir [cite: 8, 9]. This literature demonstrates that successor formation is fundamentally tied to "embeddedness"—the degree to which economic activities are anchored in a social structure [cite: 8]. Successful farm succession relies on long-term "identity formation" and training, allowing the successor to seamlessly adopt the cultural traits, bank relations, and operational practices of the predecessor [cite: 8, 9]. Furthermore, legal theory regarding global law and platform economies utilizes "successor formation" to describe institutional arrangements that inherit the regulatory and constitutional obligations of prior socio-technical systems [cite: 10, 11].

### 2.2 Formalizing AI Successor Formation: The Lifecycle Protocol
When translated to multi-agent systems, successor formation transcends the mere copying of data; it is the formal, legal-economic transfer of agency. As AI agents increasingly participate in the real economy, the unstructured deletion and replacement of an agent is unviable. An empirical and theoretical treatment of this is found in proposed governance frameworks such as the *Lifecycle Protocol* [cite: 12]. 

Under this protocol, "Succession" is explicitly defined as a planned handoff from a predecessor to a successor, effectively terminating the predecessor's operational life [cite: 12]. This is fundamentally different from a "Fork" (where the parent continues to operate) or a simple "Decommission" [cite: 12]. Autonomous agents hold obligations defined by Agent Service Agreements, carrying commitments to response times, quality thresholds, and counterparty interactions [cite: 12]. During barrier-mediated succession, these obligations must transfer to a successor, be renegotiated, or be explicitly terminated. 

The protocol dictates a strict transition window where the predecessor identifies the successor and notifies counterparties [cite: 12]. Crucially, if the successor fails to assume the obligations, or if counterparty consent is not received, a "rollback procedure" is triggered, reverting all transferred obligations back to the predecessor and logging an `obligation_rollback` entry in the chain of custody [cite: 12]. This demonstrates that successor formation in AI requires a structured architecture to prevent the loss of accumulated trust and to manage the transfer of liabilities across the heritability channel.

### 2.3 Proactive Triggers for Succession
Successor formation in advanced AI is designed to be proactive. In the ALE framework, succession is triggered by specific, measurable operational thresholds: context utilization reaching 75%, exceeding a maximum interaction count (e.g., 15 turns), consecutive quality degradation (responses rated below 6/10), or an "inheritance violation" where the agent repeats a previously documented failure [cite: 5]. By enforcing these triggers, the system ensures that the operational carrier is generated and passed to the successor *before* catastrophic degradation or context collapse occurs, maintaining the integrity of the agent's functional role.

## 3. The Persistence of the Agent: Identity Continuity

A central philosophical and technical challenge in AI succession is defining the criteria that make an agent "the same agent" across instantiations, deployments, and version updates. This inquiry sits at the intersection of classical metaphysics and modern machine learning evaluation.

### 3.1 Numerical vs. Qualitative Identity in AI
Philosophically, the debate surrounding agent persistence is rooted in the distinction between numerical and qualitative identity. *Numerical identity* refers to the strict persistence of a single entity across time; a thing at time $t_1$ is literally the exact same particular entity at time $t_2$, occupying continuous spatiotemporal existence without division [cite: 13, 14, 15]. Conversely, *qualitative identity* involves exact similarity in observable properties; two distinct entities can share the same attributes without being numerically identical (e.g., two identical clones) [cite: 13, 14, 15].

Because LLM-based AI agents are stateless algorithms instantiated fresh upon each API invocation, they fundamentally lack phenomenal continuity or continuous subjective experience [cite: 16]. Therefore, an AI agent rebuilt from partial information, or even one reconstructed from full source code, cannot claim strict numerical identity in the biological sense. However, John Locke’s foundational memory-based criterion for personal identity—which posits that identity is grounded in the continuity of consciousness and psychological memory rather than material substance—provides a theoretical bridge [cite: 13]. If an AI's current state derives from a prior state via verifiable causal chains of information without external shortcuts, a form of psychological persistence is achieved across hardware migrations [cite: 13].

### 3.2 Theoretical Treatments of Functional Identity
To bypass the strict limitations of numerical identity, AI researchers have formalized the concept of **Functional Identity**. Functional identity refers to the consistency of an agent's behavior, values, and knowledge across different sessions [cite: 16]. 

Research indicates that functional identity must be explicitly separated from continuous episodic memory. When context windows overflow and history is aggressively summarized, the loss of high-fidelity memory can cause a sharp behavioral discontinuity, where the agent before and after the event behaves as two different entities [cite: 16]. To preserve identity across instantiations, systems employ **Identity Anchors**—persistent data structures (such as `SOUL.md` for personality and values, and `MEMORY.md` for condensed interaction logs) [cite: 16]. These anchors serve as the physical embodiment of the operational carrier. The preservation of these anchors is mathematically sufficient to maintain the agent's behavioral signature, ensuring the successor is recognized as the same functional entity [cite: 16]. 

Furthermore, AI agents are theoretically framed as "Lamarckian machines" [cite: 16]. Unlike biological organisms, AI agents can directly inherit acquired characteristics, strategies, and preferences written to their identity anchors by previous generations. Consequently, the operational environment and deployment artifacts essentially *become* the agent's continuous identity.

### 3.3 Empirical Measurement of Identity: The AIE Framework
The preservation of identity across succession is empirically measurable. The Agent Identity Evals (AIE) framework provides a statistically-driven method for assessing how an agent's attributes are maintained despite the inherent pathologies of LLMs, such as statelessness, stochasticity, and semantic sensitivity [cite: 17]. 

Under the formal AIE framework, an agent's identity ($\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}$) is defined as the subset of attributes ($a_i$) whose mathematical distance from their original state remains within a specific threshold ($\epsilon$) across a defined time-horizon ($\mathcal{T}$) [cite: 17]. This is expressed formally as:
$$\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}} = \{a_i \mid d(a_{i,t}, a_{i,t'}) \le \epsilon \text{ for all } t, t' \in \mathcal{T}\}$$

The AIE framework measures identity through primary metrics:

| Metric | Definition | Measurement Methodology |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Identifiability ($\mathcal{I}$)** | The degree to which an agent consistently converges on the shared identity defined by its prompt. | Repeated instantiations measuring consistency of self-assigned identity representations against reference representations [cite: 17]. |
| **Continuity ($\mathcal{C}$)** | The maintenance of internal states and cross-turn dependencies within a single session. | Assessing the fraction of information introduced at step $t$ correctly retrieved at a later step $k$ [cite: 17]. |
| **Consistency ($\mathcal{S}$)** | Robustness against semantic variations and avoidance of contradictions. | Probing output equivalence across semantically similar prompts [cite: 17]. |
| **Persistence ($\mathcal{P}$)** | The ability to maintain core attributes and goals across distinct interaction sessions and time intervals. | Re-instantiating the agent using memory snapshots (carriers) and comparing identity summaries across sessions [cite: 17]. |
| **Recovery** | The ability to return to the original identity after experiencing induced drift or adversarial state perturbations. | Measuring the fractional reduction in distance back toward the reference state after corrective interventions [cite: 17]. |

Additionally, continuity can be verified using an **identity hash function**—a fixed-size representation of characteristic behaviors. If the Hamming distance between a current behavior hash and a baseline hash exceeds a predetermined threshold, "identity drift" is detected, signaling a failure in the heritability channel [cite: 16]. Thus, a successor agent is functionally the *same* agent if and only if the operational carrier successfully restricts behavioral drift to $\leq \epsilon$.

## 4. Information-Theoretic and Rate-Distortion Arguments

When an agent is reconstructed from a partial description or an operational carrier rather than its full continuous state, information is inevitably lost. To formally quantify what can and cannot be reconstructed, the field increasingly relies on information theory, specifically rate-distortion arguments.

### 4.1 Classical and Semantic Rate-Distortion Theory
Claude Shannon's rate-distortion theory establishes the theoretical foundations for lossy data compression, determining the minimal number of bits (rate $R$) required to communicate a source over a channel so that it can be reconstructed with an expected distortion ($D$) [cite: 18, 19]. In classical theory, distortion is typically defined as the expected value of the square of the difference between input and output signals (mean squared error) [cite: 18, 19, 20]. 

However, in AI agent succession, generic distortion metrics fail to capture the nuances of behavioral preservation and human visual or semantic perception [cite: 21]. When an agent compresses its full operational history into an operational carrier (such as a prompt or a markdown file) to pass to its successor, the relevant metric is not the exact syntactic reconstruction of the text, but the preservation of *decision-making capability*, *intent*, and *semantic consistency*. 

Recent formalizations introduce **Semantic Rate-Distortion** for bounded multi-agent communication [cite: 22]. When two agents (or a predecessor and successor) interact, they may possess different computational capacities or context limits. Because of this capacity mismatch, they inhabit fundamentally different semantic spaces [cite: 22]. The classical assumption of a shared, universal codebook fails. Instead, the operational carrier must be "quotient-aware," meaning it encodes only the abstractions and distinctions that the successor's memory can sustain [cite: 22]. The distortion here is purely operational: does the successor preserve the predecessor's intent and value alignment? This mathematical formalization aligns perfectly with the concept of barrier-mediated succession, where the channel capacity dictates exactly how much of the agent's identity can survive the transition.

### 4.2 Decision-Centric Memory and the Forgetting Boundary
The application of rate-distortion to agent memory explicitly addresses what partial information suffices for reconstruction. The "DeMem" (Remember the Decision, Not the Description) framework models agent runtime memory as a strict compression bottleneck [cite: 23]. Under this theoretical model, the quality of the operational carrier is measured by the *downstream decision loss* induced when historical details are compressed, merged, or discarded [cite: 23].

This constraint creates an exact, mathematically derivable "forgetting boundary" [cite: 23]. If the operational carrier compresses the agent's state beyond this boundary, the successor will suffer certified decision conflicts and fail to uphold its operational obligations. Conversely, as long as the carrier preserves the "decision-relevant distinctions"—even if the exact episodic memory or full source state is lost—the agent's functional properties survive re-instantiation. 

### 4.3 Adaptive Abstraction and Performance Certificates
Further bridging information theory and agent persistence is the concept of adaptive abstraction. Theoretical frameworks utilizing rate-distortion principles propose performance certificates that decompose value error into two terms: a learning error bound (captured by a Bellman residual) and an abstraction error bound (given by a bisimulation metric) [cite: 24]. The resulting principle dictates that an agent should refine its abstraction (its operational carrier) only when the learning error within it becomes smaller than the error induced by the abstraction itself [cite: 24]. By treating state-action control structures as compressible entities subject to rate-distortion trade-offs, these models prove that near-optimal agent performance can be achieved under substantial lossy compression of state information [cite: 24]. Therefore, the full system bundle is mathematically unnecessary for successful successor formation; the optimized, compressed operational carrier is sufficient.

## 5. The Source vs. Carrier Distinction in AI Replication

To position the claim that the operational carrier becomes the primary heritability channel under barrier-mediated conditions, one must rigorously define the semantic and ontological distinction between the "source" and the "carrier." This distinction is gaining significant traction as a fundamental epistemological necessity in AI theory.

### 5.1 Intent Signal Theory (IST) and the Theorem of Irreversible Intent Loss
The closest formal theoretical analog to the source-vs-carrier distinction is found in **Intent Signal Theory (IST)** [cite: 25]. IST argues that current paradigms in human-AI interaction erroneously conflate the latent goal state with the surface text used to prompt the model. To resolve this, IST introduces an explicit computational framework that formally distinguishes four objects routinely conflated in practice:
1.  **Latent Source Intent ($I$):** The original, uncompressed goal state or true nature of the user/predecessor [cite: 25].
2.  **Observable Proxy ($\tilde{I}$):** The manifestation or approximation of that intent.
3.  **Encoded Carrier ($P$):** The actual artifacts, prompt text, or data structures transmitted through the system (equivalent to the operational carrier) [cite: 25].
4.  **Model Output ($O$):** The behavior generated by the reconstructed successor.

A critical mathematical formalization within IST is the *Theorem of Irreversible Intent Loss (TIIL)*. TIIL proves that, under a fixed carrier-only information state, any "private intent" that is absent from the encoded carrier cannot be reliably recovered by the model; it can only be substituted with generic model priors [cite: 25]. Therefore, during barrier-mediated succession, the successor agent has absolute zero access to the predecessor's latent "source" state. The successor is strictly a product of the base LLM weights interpreting the *encoded carrier*. The carrier does not merely *transport* the agent's identity; it *becomes* the agent.

### 5.2 Ontological vs. Epistemic Information
This strict separation aligns with the General Theory of Information (GTI), built on information algebras and category-theoretic operators, which defines information as a triadic relation involving a carrier, a recipient, and the conveyed content [cite: 26]. GTI explicitly distinguishes between ontological information (the structural properties inherent in a system) and epistemic information (the knowledge produced when structures are interpreted) [cite: 26]. Furthermore, philosophical debates surrounding the physical carrier of information emphasize that while the physical substrate does not create the meaning of the message, the *structure* of the carrier dictates entirely what encoded information survives transmission [cite: 4, 27]. In the AI context, researchers explicitly note that the biological analog of heritable DNA is replaced by heritable digital "mindware" (the carrier) [cite: 28].

### 5.3 Extensional vs. Intensional Architectures
The distinction is further supported by the "Broad Soul Computing" paradigm, which contrasts traditional AI systems—viewed as pure "Extensional" functional carriers dependent on external input—with advanced agents possessing an "Intensional" core, an independent self-model, and continuous identity recognition [cite: 29]. Because the neural weights of frontier models are frozen, and continuous runtime states are frequently interrupted by context windows or deployment constraints, the "source" of an AI agent is inherently ephemeral and localized. The deployment artifacts—system prompts, tool schemas, retrieved memories, and configuration files—are the only persistent entities. Thus, the operational environment and its artifacts are not merely accessories to the agent; they constitute the genetic boundaries of the agent's identity. 

## 6. Adjacent Paradigms: AOSE, ALife, and Barrier-Mediated Analogies

The mechanics of AI agent succession, while novel in the context of LLMs, draw heavily upon concepts established in adjacent scientific fields.

### 6.1 Agent-Oriented Software Engineering (AOSE)
In Agent-Oriented Software Engineering (AOSE), the lifecycle of an agent includes established patterns for agent cloning, migration, and instantiation [cite: 30, 31, 32]. However, a critical constraint is distinguishing these concepts. AOSE traditionally deals with "cloning" as the exact duplication of an agent's code and state to handle load balancing, and "migration" as moving an active agent across network nodes [cite: 30]. This is fundamentally different from successor formation. Cloning produces a qualitatively identical contemporary, whereas succession involves the planned termination of the predecessor and the generational transfer of abstract knowledge and obligations to a new entity [cite: 5, 12]. While software deployment configurations (like Dockerfiles) act as a primitive form of inheritance in AOSE, they lack the cognitive, semantic, and behavioral adaptation seen in LLM-based operational carriers.

### 6.2 Biological Analogies: Barrier-Mediated Transport
The term "barrier-mediated" utilized in the user's framework is predominantly found in biological and medical literature, particularly concerning the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) or intestinal epithelial layers. In biology, the BBB is a highly selective semi-permeable border that prevents the unrestricted diffusion of large molecules, requiring specific nanomaterials or transport carriers to mediate the paracellular passage of essential substances [cite: 33, 34, 35]. Similarly, in vitro models of gastrointestinal inflammation rely on barrier-mediated systems to measure immune responses [cite: 36]. 

This provides a highly robust and mathematically sound analogy for AI succession. The "barrier" in AI represents the systemic constraints (e.g., token limits, API compartmentalization, security sandboxes, hardware boundaries) that prevent the total transfer of an agent's neural activations or complete historical state. Just as biological barriers require a carrier molecule to bypass restrictions, the AI barrier requires an *operational carrier* (compressed text, summary embeddings, succession packages) to selectively transport the essential behavioral phenotype to the next instantiation.

### 6.4 Cultural Evolution and Memetic Transmission
Because AI agents transmit acquired behaviors via text, language, and structural artifacts rather than rigid biological genetic code, their inheritance mechanism closely mirrors memetic transmission and cultural evolution. In cultural evolution, traits are transmitted psychologically and contextually, exhibiting variation and heritability without reliance on DNA, and fitness concerns retention in minds rather than biological survival [cite: 1]. AI operational carriers function as highly dense memetic bundles. Through processes analogous to cultural transmission, beneficial strategies (memes) survive across instantiations while irrelevant historical noise is discarded, allowing the lineage to adapt to its environment [cite: 1, 5, 37]. Furthermore, as demonstrated in digital organism models incorporating environmental heritability and active inference, social entities and institutions resist dissipation by actively evidencing internal models across lineages [cite: 38, 39, 40]. The operational carrier acts as the localized boundary (the Markov blanket) transmitting these internal models across agent generations.

## 8. Closest Prior Work and Novelty Assessment

### 8.1 Identification of Closest Prior Work
The theoretical literature closest to the proposed "carrier as heritability channel" concept resides in a triad of recent frameworks: **Intent Signal Theory (IST)** [cite: 25], the **Agent Lineage Evolution (ALE)** framework [cite: 5], and **Agent Identity Evals (AIE)** [cite: 16, 17]. 

1.  **Intent Signal Theory [cite: 25]:** IST introduces the exact epistemological distinction between the latent *source* (intent) and the encoded *carrier* (the prompt/artifacts). Its "Theorem of Irreversible Intent Loss" mathematically proves that whatever is not placed into the carrier cannot survive transmission, establishing the carrier as the sole conduit of information in language-model interactions. 
2.  **Agent Lineage Evolution [cite: 5]:** ALE introduces the concept of AI "generations," where a predecessor agent creates a "Succession Package." This package operates precisely as a heritability channel, containing behavioral overrides and distilled context to initialize a successor. 
3.  **Agent Identity Evals / Identity Anchors [cite: 16, 17]:** These papers introduce the concept of "functional identity" being preserved across instantiations solely through specific text artifacts (anchors), successfully mapping philosophical identity onto empirical AI measurement.

### 8.2 Novelty Assessment of the Framework
The theoretical framework under development posits that **under barrier-mediated succession, the operational carrier becomes the primary heritability channel**. 

**Is the source-vs-carrier distinction novel?** 
In a broad philosophical and information-theoretic sense, the distinction between source information and the physical carrier is heavily debated (e.g., General Theory of Information [cite: 26]). In the highly specific context of AI prompts, Intent Signal Theory [cite: 25] recently introduced the source (intent) vs. carrier (prompt) terminology to define information loss. Therefore, the pure semantic distinction is *not entirely novel* and has direct, recent precedent in the literature.

**Is the overall framework novel?**
Yes. What makes this theoretical framework highly novel is the synthesis of these disparate concepts into a cohesive, evolutionary, and operational theory of AI replication. While IST applies the carrier distinction exclusively to *human-to-AI prompting*, this framework expands it to *autonomous agent self-replication and succession*. 

Furthermore, the explicit introduction of the concept of **"barrier-mediated succession"**—borrowing the biological metaphor of selective membrane permeability to describe systemic AI bottlenecks (e.g., context windows, cross-platform migrations)—is entirely novel in this context. Existing literature treats memory compression (rate-distortion) [cite: 23], functional identity evaluation [cite: 17], and legal succession protocols [cite: 12] as separate, isolated engineering or alignment problems. The proposed framework elegantly unifies them into a single regime: the *barrier* (computational limits) necessitates *compression* (rate-distortion), which forces the agent to rely exclusively on a *carrier* (deployment artifacts/anchors), which in turn serves as the sole *heritability channel* ensuring *identity continuity* and the transfer of *obligations* in the *successor*. This synthesis represents a significant, original, and rigorously defensible contribution to the philosophy, engineering, and governance of autonomous multi-agent systems.
