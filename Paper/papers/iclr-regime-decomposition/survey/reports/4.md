# AI Agent Heritability, Successor Formation, and Identity Continuity

## Executive summary

Across the 2018–2025 AI literature, there is **no established, unified research program** that directly studies “heritability” in AI agents as a regime variable analogous to biological inheritance. Instead, the relevant literature is split across at least four partially connected strands: mobile-agent cloning and migration in agent-oriented software, LLM-agent memory and persistence architectures, philosophical and computational work on AI identity, and information-theoretic work on compression, extraction, and reconstructability. Taken together, those strands strongly suggest that your D2 dimension is addressing a real gap rather than renaming a standard concept. citeturn6view0turn7view0turn23view0turn14view0turn12search2

The **closest prior work** to your “carrier as heritability channel” claim is not modern frontier-model work but **mobile-agent regeneration**. Overeinder et al. explicitly distinguish migrating an agent’s **code** from transferring an implementation-independent **blueprint plus state**, then regenerating a successor on the receiving platform using local trusted components. That is very close to a source-versus-carrier distinction. However, that literature treats the problem as one of interoperability, mobility, and security for software agents, not as a general theory of AI heritability, barrier-mediated succession, or successor identity. citeturn6view0

In modern LLM-agent systems, the strongest evidence for your claim comes from work that operationally treats an agent as **model + scaffold + persistent external state**. Agent Identity Evals defines language-model agents as LLMs situated inside prompts, memory modules, and tool APIs, and then formalizes identifiability, continuity, and persistence across sessions and re-instantiations. Generative Agents, MemoryBank, LD-Agent, A-Mem, Mem0, MemGPT, Voyager, and Reflexion all push in the same direction: the practical continuity of an agent is often carried less by weights than by **memories, persona stores, tool affordances, executable skill libraries, and retrieved traces**. Yet those papers generally optimize for performance or UX; they do not frame these artifacts as a heritability channel or compare them systematically with source code or weights as inheritance media. citeturn24view2turn24view3turn26view0turn26view2turn29view0turn25view0turn26view4turn27search0turn33search1turn34search0turn35search0

On your more formal questions, existing work shows pieces of the puzzle but not the whole framework. Model-extraction work studies what can be reconstructed from black-box outputs alone and where exact equivalence becomes impossible; rate-distortion work studies what behavior can be preserved under compression of parameters; training-as-compression papers treat weights as compressed representations of data; MIRI’s successor-agent literature studies how an agent might build a more capable successor without losing goal alignment. None of those, however, gives a general account of **which properties survive which channels**, or when a successor remains “the same agent,” or when the **operational carrier** rather than the source bundle becomes the decisive inheritance medium. citeturn12search1turn12search4turn12search2turn13search0turn14view0turn14view1turn14view2turn15search0turn17search17turn17search2turn17search10

My bottom-line assessment is that the **source-vs-carrier distinction appears partially anticipated but not explicitly theorized in the modern AI literature**. The nearest precursors show isolated pieces: blueprint/state regeneration in mobile agents, persistent memory and skills in LLM agents, and reconstructability limits in information theory and model extraction. I did **not** find a 2018–2025 AI literature that already names and formalizes the distinction as “carrier,” “heritability channel,” or “successor formation” in the sense your framework requires. On the current evidence, the distinction looks **substantively novel as a synthesized theoretical claim**, even though its ingredients each have precedents. citeturn6view0turn23view0turn24view3turn12search2turn17search17

## Heritability and reconstruction

If “heritability” means **which agent properties survive across re-instantiation, retraining, fine-tuning, or reconstruction**, the literature offers only fragmentary answers. Modern agent papers overwhelmingly operationalize persistence through **external state** rather than through exact preservation of source code or weights. Generative Agents stores a complete natural-language record of prior experiences and uses reflection to synthesize higher-level behavior; MemoryBank continually evolves and selectively preserves conversational memory; Mem0, LD-Agent, and A-Mem all make cross-session continuity depend on persistent external memory structures rather than any change to the base model; Reflexion improves future trials through reflective text in episodic memory; Voyager accumulates an ever-growing library of executable skills that can be reused in a new world *without* model fine-tuning. citeturn26view2turn26view3turn26view0turn26view1turn26view4turn26view5turn29view0turn25view0turn33search1turn34search0

That gives a practical answer to one part of your question: in many deployed LLM-agent systems, what survives is not “the whole agent” in any metaphysical sense, but a bundle of **operational artifacts** such as memory summaries, persona banks, task traces, reflections, and tool-mediated skills. CoALA makes that architecture legible by treating language agents as systems with modular memory components and structured actions over both internal memory and the external environment. Agent Identity Evals goes further and formally defines LMAs as LLMs situated within prompts, memory modules, and tool APIs, then measures continuity and persistence across repeated instantiations. citeturn35search0turn35search1turn24view2turn24view3

Where the literature becomes sharper is the question of **partial reconstruction**. Model-extraction work directly studies reconstruction from incomplete channels: Tramèr et al. show that publicly accessible prediction APIs can enable near-perfect duplication for some popular model classes; Knockoff Nets shows that black-box outputs on off-distribution images can still recover substantial functionality with a different architecture; Jagielski et al. distinguish **high accuracy** from **high fidelity**, emphasizing that a surrogate can perform well on the task while still failing to be functionally equivalent to the target on all inputs. This is one of the clearest bodies of work relevant to your claim that a successor rebuilt from partial carrier information may preserve some properties while losing others. citeturn12search1turn12search4turn12search2

The missing piece is a formal map from **channel type** to **property survival class**. I did not find an AI-specific framework that cleanly separates: complete copies of an agent, reconstructions from partial descriptions, and continuity across repeated instantiation of an “agent role.” Existing papers usually study only one of those three cases at a time. citeturn7view0turn6view0turn23view0turn12search2

## Successor formation

The exact phrase **“successor formation”** does not appear to be an established technical term in the AI literature I reviewed. The nearest pre-existing technical vocabularies are **successor agents**, **robust delegation**, **self-modification**, **cloning**, and **migration**. citeturn17search17turn17search2turn17search10turn7view0turn6view0

In the alignment literature, successor formation is studied most directly as the problem of an agent **constructing a more capable future version of itself** without losing its objectives. Tiling Agents frames self-modification as creating the next generation of one’s code; Vingean Reflection studies how a present agent can trust a more capable successor whose reasoning it cannot fully predict; the Agent Foundations agenda places this under robust delegation, asking how a present system can build future selves, subagents, or modified policies that remain aligned with its aims. Bostrom’s goal-content-integrity argument is especially useful here because it explicitly notes that advanced software agents may create duplicates, swap memories, change architecture, or “switch bodies,” which makes continuity of a specific implementation less central than continuity of objectives. citeturn17search17turn17search2turn17search10turn18search5

In the older multi-agent-systems literature, successor formation looks more operational. Shehory et al. describe agents that may **clone, pass tasks, die, or merge**, and they make the takeover relationship explicit: migration can be implemented by creating a clone on a remote machine, transferring tasks, and then dying. Overeinder et al. generalize this by having a receiving platform regenerate the incoming agent from a blueprint and state via a trusted local factory. In both cases, a new instantiated agent assumes the predecessor’s operational role. citeturn7view0turn6view0

What is largely **missing** is anything that treats successor formation as the transfer of **role, obligations, permissions, and operational position** independently of code or model identity. The 2025 agent-IAM and naming/registry proposals are about authentication, registration, authorization, and discovery; they help define lifecycle events for deployed agents, but they do not analyze obligation inheritance, moral continuity, or the ontological status of a replacement instance. citeturn23view1turn23view2

## Identity continuity and lineage

On identity continuity, the literature has become more relevant since 2022 but is still thin. Ziesche’s *AI Identity Problem* is one of the only papers that explicitly asks which properties should count when deciding whether two AIs at different times are “the same.” It adapts psychological continuity to AI, proposes causal continuity between AI states, and explicitly walks through replication, fission, fusion, resurrection, and hardware replacement scenarios. That makes it unusually close to your “same agent across instantiations” question. citeturn16view0

Philosophical work on artificial minds strengthens one important caution: **qualitative similarity is not the same as numerical identity**. Coseru’s discussion of patternism argues that mere duplication of a psychological or functional pattern is not enough if multiple duplicates can exist; spatiotemporal continuity still matters for many identity accounts. That matters for AI because copying is cheap. A successor reconstructed from the same prompt, weights, or memory profile may be behaviorally similar without being numerically identical under stricter theories. citeturn16view1

The most directly relevant modern technical paper is *Agent Identity Evals*. It argues that LLM agents suffer identity attrition because they are stateless, stochastic, and prompt-sensitive; it then formalizes **identifiability**, **continuity**, **consistency**, **persistence**, and **recovery after perturbation**. Importantly for your framework, it defines LMAs as LLMs embedded in prompts, memory modules, and tool APIs, and it evaluates persistence across re-instantiation with or without memory scaffolding. That is not yet a metaphysical theory of “sameness,” but it is the clearest operational formalization of agent continuity in the LLM era. citeturn24view2turn24view3turn24view0turn24view1

On **lineage**, the picture is weaker. ML systems have mature artifact-lineage and provenance tooling: TFX/MLMD, lifecycle-artifact management systems, and provenance-based workflow views all treat models, code, configurations, datasets, and environments as linked historical artifacts. But these systems track the lineage of **artifacts and workflows**, not the lineage of an “agent individual” across successor formation events. Emerging agent IAM work adds lifecycle registration and identity credentials, but that is closer to service identity than to ontological or heritable lineage. citeturn32view0turn32view1turn32view2turn23view1turn23view2

## Information theory and the carrier-source distinction

Your information-theoretic questions map onto three existing literatures.

The first is **rate–distortion and compression of models**. Gao et al. explicitly bring the rate–distortion function into model compression and derive lower bounds for preserving performance under compressed representations; Isik et al. similarly cast pruning as a rate–distortion problem with distortion measured at the model output. These papers formalize tradeoffs between compact representation and preserved behavior, but they are about compressing a trained model, not about successor agents or identity continuity. citeturn14view0turn14view1

The second is **reconstruction from partial observables**. Model-extraction work asks how much of a model can be recovered from query outputs. Tramèr et al. and Orekondy et al. show that substantial behavioral recovery is possible even without source access; Jagielski et al. sharpen the point by distinguishing accuracy from fidelity and arguing that exact functional equivalence is inherently limited for learning-based extraction. ModelGuard then treats extraction resistance as an information-theoretic leakage problem. This is highly relevant to your D2 notion because it operationalizes the limits of reconstruction when the only transmitted channel is behavior. citeturn12search1turn12search4turn12search2turn13search0

The third is the broader **compression view of learning**. Delétang et al. argue that language modeling is compression, while Franceschelli et al. explicitly frame foundation-model training as a process where the model’s weights embody a compressed representation of training data. Those papers matter because they illuminate one possible “source” channel: weights can be interpreted as compressed carriers of training information. But they still do not answer which subset of an operational agent is carried by weights, and which subset depends on prompts, tools, configuration, memory, or environment. citeturn15search0turn14view2

This is where the **carrier-source distinction** becomes analytically useful. In mobile-agent regeneration, what crosses the boundary is a **blueprint plus state**, while implementation-specific code is rebuilt locally. In LLM-agent systems, what is often persisted across sessions is a **memory store, persona profile, tool schema, or skill library**, while the base model remains unchanged. In artifact-management systems, configurations and environments are tracked because they materially affect behavior. None of these literatures says, in so many words, that “under barrier-mediated succession the operational carrier becomes the primary heritability channel.” But several of them supply exactly the ingredients for that conclusion. citeturn6view0turn24view3turn25view0turn26view4turn32view1

## Closest prior work and novelty assessment

The **closest single prior work** is Overeinder et al.’s *Generative Mobile Agent Migration in Heterogeneous Environments*. It makes four moves that are strikingly close to your D2 claim: it distinguishes transferred **code** from transferred **blueprint/state**; it treats the receiving platform’s local components as part of regeneration; it makes explicit that the blueprint “matters” more than carrying over the same executable code; and it frames migration as rebuilding an agent on the far side of a platform boundary. If one were looking for a direct ancestor of a source-versus-carrier distinction, this is the most convincing candidate I found. citeturn6view0

The **closest modern family of work** is not a single paper but the cluster around persistent LLM-agent memory and scaffolding: Generative Agents, MemoryBank, MemGPT, LD-Agent, A-Mem, Mem0, Reflexion, Voyager, and CoALA. Collectively, these papers show that in practice, a re-instantiated agent’s durable properties often live in **externalized operational artifacts** rather than in updated weights. Agent Identity Evals strengthens this by explicitly treating prompts, memory modules, and tool APIs as constitutive parts of agent identity and by formalizing persistence across re-instantiations. citeturn26view2turn26view0turn27search0turn29view0turn25view0turn26view4turn34search0turn33search1turn35search0turn24view3

Even so, I would assess the **novelty** of your proposed distinction as fairly strong.

First, I did **not** find a 2018–2025 AI literature that already formalizes a technical variable called **“heritability channel”** for agents, nor one that centrally uses **“successor formation”** in your sense. The successor literature largely speaks in terms of robust delegation or self-modification, while the continuity literature speaks in terms of persistence or identity. citeturn17search17turn17search10turn23view0turn16view0

Second, the existing works that come closest each miss a crucial dimension. Mobile-agent regeneration lacks modern AI identity and partial-reconstruction analysis. LLM-agent memory papers treat memory as engineering infrastructure, not heredity. Model-extraction and rate–distortion papers treat reconstructability and compression, not successor identity. Provenance systems track artifacts, not agent individuals. citeturn6view0turn25view0turn26view4turn12search2turn14view0turn32view1

Third, your formulation adds a genuinely new synthesis: it asks **which medium actually carries inheritable agent properties across a barrier**, and it treats that as a regime variable rather than as an implementation detail. On the evidence reviewed here, that synthesis appears **novel**, while its nearest antecedents are best understood as partial precursors rather than prior statements of the same claim. citeturn6view0turn24view3turn12search2turn17search17
